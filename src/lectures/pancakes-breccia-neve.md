<p class="lec-standfirst">Ice is the defining landscape of the polar regions, and yet for most visitors arriving in the Arctic or the Southern Ocean for the first time, “ice” is a single word for what is in fact a vocabulary. There is the ice that rests on land and the ice that forms on the sea; the ice that has stood for hundreds of thousands of years and the ice that came into being last week. There is pancake ice, ice breccia, névé, brash, nilas, shuga, floebergs and floebits. Each of these names points to a specific physical process, a specific stage in a life cycle, and very often to a specific kind of risk or opportunity for anything that wants to move through it. This is a tour of that vocabulary — grounded in numbers, but written for anyone preparing to spend time among ice rather than only reading about it.</p>

<hr class="lec-rule" />

## Two poles, two worlds

Roughly ten percent of the Earth's surface is permanently covered by ice, and the great majority of that ice sits at the two ends of the planet. About ten percent of the global ice mass is in the Arctic, just under ninety percent is locked in Antarctica, and the small remainder is distributed across the high mountains of the world — the Himalaya, the Andes, the European Alps, the Southern Alps of New Zealand, the volcanoes of Iceland.

That single sentence is, in some ways, the entire problem in miniature. The two polar regions look superficially alike from a satellite — both are white — but they are radically different systems, and treating them as mirror images is the first mistake to avoid.

### The Arctic: an ocean surrounded by land

The Arctic is, fundamentally, an ocean. Ice sits in two very different reservoirs. On land — most of it on Greenland, with smaller contributions from the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, Svalbard, the Russian Arctic islands and Iceland — the Arctic holds approximately 3.1 million cubic kilometres of ice, with ice thicknesses reaching up to roughly three kilometres at the centre of the Greenland ice sheet.<span class="lec-cite">(1)</span>

The other reservoir is the sea ice that forms on the surface of the Arctic Ocean itself. Its area swings dramatically with the seasons, but its average annual extent of around nine million square kilometres is itself larger than the entire continent of Europe. The volume, however, is comparatively small — around twenty thousand cubic kilometres — because the ice is rarely more than five metres thick. Average thickness in the central Arctic basin is closer to two or three metres, with pressure ridges occasionally reaching twenty metres where wind and current have crushed floes against one another. These figures are themselves models, built up from satellite altimetry and a relatively sparse network of in-situ measurements; they should be treated as best estimates rather than precise facts.

### Antarctica: a continent surrounded by ocean

Antarctica is the inverse: a continent buried under ice, ringed by a circumpolar ocean. The Antarctic Ice Sheet contains roughly 26.5 million cubic kilometres of ice — about ninety percent of the freshwater on Earth — and reaches thicknesses of up to 4,776 metres.<span class="lec-cite">(2)</span> That is nearly five kilometres of ice piled in one place. If the entire Antarctic ice sheet were to melt, global sea level would rise by approximately 58 metres.

Antarctic sea ice behaves differently from its Arctic counterpart for the simple reason that there is no land to confine it. In the austral summer it shrinks to around three million square kilometres, hugging the coast; by the September peak it has expanded to roughly eighteen million square kilometres, doubling the apparent size of the continent. Average thickness is generally only one to two metres, half that of the Arctic, because most of the ice melts each year and reforms from scratch the following winter.

## The fundamental distinction

Before going further, the most important conceptual division: land ice and sea ice are not the same substance, and they do not behave the same way.

Land ice forms from fresh water. Snow falls on a glacier or ice sheet, layer accumulates upon layer, and over years and centuries the lower layers are compacted under the weight of those above into solid, almost air-free ice. The process is slow, the ice that results can persist for hundreds of thousands of years, and the timescales involved are essentially geological.

Sea ice forms from salt water. The top layer of the ocean cools to its freezing point, ice crystals begin to grow, and within hours a skin of new ice can extend across the surface. Most sea ice lives a single year and is gone again. Even the oldest multi-year ice in the Arctic basin is, in geological terms, brand new.

### Albedo and the feedback that runs the polar regions

<figure class="lec-fig lec-fig--side"><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><figcaption>Schematic of the ice-albedo feedback. <span class="lec-fig__src">Henry Páll Wulff.</span></figcaption></figure>

Both kinds of ice share one property that has outsized consequences: they are white, which means they have a high albedo. Roughly eighty percent of the sunlight that strikes intact sea ice is reflected back into space. When that ice melts and exposes the dark ocean beneath, the same surface absorbs around ninety percent of incoming sunlight instead. The water warms, which in turn melts more ice, which exposes more water, which absorbs more sunlight. This is a positive feedback — a self-amplifying loop — and it is one of the principal reasons that polar warming runs ahead of the global average, and one of the reasons climate projections in the Arctic in particular are so difficult to constrain.

## Land ice

Land ice can be classified, with reasonable consistency, by size. The terminology is hierarchical: ice sheets are the largest, ice caps smaller, glaciers smaller still, and ice fields a special case constrained by terrain. Most of what follows is concerned with the first three.

### Ice sheets

An ice sheet — sometimes called a continental glacier — is a mass of glacial ice greater than fifty thousand square kilometres. By that strict definition there are only two ice sheets on Earth at present: the Antarctic Ice Sheet and the Greenland Ice Sheet. During the Last Glacial Maximum, around twenty thousand years ago, there were several more: the Laurentide Ice Sheet across most of North America, the Weichselian Ice Sheet across northern Europe, and the Patagonian Ice Sheet in southern South America. They are gone now, and what remains of them — the Greenland Ice Sheet aside — are the smaller bodies that survived in cold pockets after the rest melted away.

In geological terms, the present polar ice sheets are not ancient. The Antarctic Ice Sheet first formed as a small ice cap roughly thirty-four million years ago and went through repeated cycles of advance and retreat before settling into something close to its present configuration during the Pliocene, around three million years ago. The Greenland Ice Sheet did not exist at all until the late Pliocene; it built up rapidly thereafter, with the curious side effect that fossils of the plants once growing in southern Greenland are unusually well preserved beneath the ice that buried them.

### The Antarctic Ice Sheet

The Antarctic Ice Sheet is the largest single mass of ice on Earth. It covers around fourteen million square kilometres, contains 26.5 million cubic kilometres of ice, and accounts for approximately ninety percent of the world's freshwater. Its full melting — a hypothetical, not a forecast — would raise global sea level by 58 metres.

The continent does not warm uniformly. The interior and East Antarctica have shown a moderate warming of roughly 0.1 ± 0.2 °C per decade since the late 1950s, much of which falls within the noise of natural variability. The Antarctic Peninsula, by contrast, is one of the most rapidly warming regions on the planet, with temperature increases exceeding 0.3 °C per decade across the same period.<span class="lec-cite">(3)</span> That asymmetry — a relatively stable East, a destabilising West and Peninsula — is a recurring theme in the science, and it shapes almost every projection of future ice loss.

### The Greenland Ice Sheet

<figure class="lec-fig lec-fig--side"><div class="lec-fig__row lec-fig__row--2"><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div></div><figcaption>Greenland maps showing (a) land topography and (b) ice bed thickness. <span class="lec-fig__src">Morlighem et al. 2017.</span></figcaption></figure>

The Greenland Ice Sheet covers around eighty-two percent of Greenland's land surface — roughly 1.8 million square kilometres — and contains approximately 2.85 million cubic kilometres of ice. It is some 2,400 kilometres long from north to south and reaches 1,100 kilometres across at its widest, near 77°N. Mean elevation of the ice surface is 2,135 metres. The ice is generally more than two kilometres thick, exceeding three kilometres at its thickest point.

Greenland's ice sheet is currently losing mass at a rate of roughly 240 cubic kilometres per year, a figure that has approximately tripled since the 1990s. If it were to melt entirely, sea level would rise by approximately 7.2 metres. The full melt is, again, not a forecast — it would take centuries at current rates — but the trajectory is unambiguous.<span class="lec-cite">(4)</span>

### Ice caps

An ice cap is essentially a small ice sheet — the same kind of body, but covering less than fifty thousand square kilometres. Iceland's Vatnajökull is an ice cap. So is Austfonna on Nordaustlandet in Svalbard, and so are the various caps on the Russian Arctic islands and the Canadian Arctic. An ice cap typically has a central dome over the highest point of the underlying massif, and its ice flows outward from that dome to feed a series of glaciers around its periphery. Unlike ice fields, ice caps are not constrained by topography — they sit on top of the landscape rather than between its features.

### Glaciers

A glacier is the smallest unit in this hierarchy and, in some ways, the most interesting. The technical definition is straightforward: a persistent body of dense ice that moves under its own weight, formed where the long-term accumulation of snow exceeds the long-term loss to melting and sublimation. Glacial ice is, collectively, the largest reservoir of freshwater on Earth, and many of the world's rivers — from the Indus to the Rhine to the Rhône — are fed in part by glacier meltwater released through the warm months.

### How snow becomes ice

On a glacier's upper reaches — the accumulation zone — more snow falls each winter than melts each summer. Layer is deposited on layer, and the lower layers are progressively compressed by the weight of those above. The transformation from snow to glacial ice is not a phase change in the usual sense; it is a slow expulsion of air.

Fresh snow is roughly ninety percent air. As it accumulates and compresses it becomes granular snow, around fifty percent air. With further compaction it becomes firn — the German term, used universally in glaciology, for old snow that has survived at least one melt season. The French equivalent, névé, appears in the title of this lecture. Firn contains roughly twenty-five percent air. Continued compression eventually closes the connecting channels between air pockets, and what remains is glacial ice — fine-grained at first, with the residual air sealed inside as bubbles, and ultimately coarse-grained ice that may contain less than twenty percent air, locked in entirely. Those bubbles preserve a sample of the atmosphere at the moment the ice formed, which is why ice cores from Antarctica and Greenland are among the most important climate archives we have.

In the deep interior of a major ice sheet, the ice at depth may be hundreds of thousands of years old. The sequence is not metaphorical: a single column of ice contains the recent past at the surface, the Last Glacial Maximum a few hundred metres down, and atmospheres far older than human civilisation at depth.

### How a glacier moves

Only moving ice is properly called a glacier. This excludes free-floating ice such as icebergs and pack ice, even though both are derived from glacier ice in the case of bergs. There are two basic mechanisms by which glaciers move.

The first is **internal deformation**, sometimes called distortion flow. Ice, although solid, behaves as a viscous fluid on long timescales. At the molecular level it is composed of layered hexagonal lattices held together by relatively weak hydrogen bonds, and when sufficient stress is applied — the weight of the ice above, exerting pressure forward and downward — those layers slide past one another. The result is a slow, plastic creep of the entire mass. Because friction at the bed and along the valley walls slows the ice in contact with rock, internal deformation produces a characteristic flow pattern: faster at the surface than at the base, and faster in the centre of the glacier than at the sides.

The second mechanism is **basal sliding**, and it requires liquid water at the bed. This sounds counterintuitive in a body of ice, but pressure changes the equation. The melting point of ice falls by roughly 0.07 °C for every hundred metres of overlying ice load — the so-called pressure melting point. A temperate glacier five hundred metres thick can have ice at its base sitting at −0.35 °C and yet, under that pressure, behaving as if it were at the melting point. A thin film of meltwater forms, and the entire glacier slips along that film. As it slips, pressure is briefly released, the water refreezes, and the cycle begins again.

Basal sliding occurs only at temperate glaciers — those whose ice is everywhere close to the melting point. Cold-based glaciers, frozen to their beds, move only by internal deformation, and they move slowly. The fast-flowing outlet glaciers and ice streams of Antarctica and Greenland are fast precisely because they are sliding on a wet bed, and the supply of meltwater to that bed is one of the variables that climate change is altering.

### The world's glaciers, mapped

Outside the two ice sheets, the global glacierised area totals approximately 726,000 square kilometres, distributed unevenly across nineteen recognised regions. The largest concentrations are in the subantarctic and Antarctic peripheral glaciers (around 133,000 km²), the Canadian Arctic North (around 105,000 km²) and High Mountain Asia (around 63,000 km²). Roughly forty-four percent of the world's non-ice-sheet glacier area is in Arctic regions and a further eighteen percent in the Antarctic and subantarctic. The low latitudes — the tropics — contain only about 2,300 km² of glacier ice, and that small total is shrinking fast.

## Sea ice

Sea ice is the more dynamic of the two systems. Land ice changes on the timescale of years and decades; sea ice changes on the timescale of hours and days. A floe that exists this morning may be a pressure ridge by evening if wind, current and the geometry of surrounding ice conspire against it. The vocabulary used to describe sea ice is correspondingly more elaborate, and it is standardised internationally through the World Meteorological Organization's sea-ice nomenclature.

What follows is the WMO classification, which is the working language of every ice service, polar mariner and serious expedition operator.<span class="lec-cite">(5)</span>

### How sea ice forms

Salt water freezes differently from fresh water. Fresh water reaches its maximum density at 4 °C and freezes at 0 °C; as a body of fresh water cools, the densest water sinks and the surface eventually freezes once the entire column is below 4 °C. Salt water has no such density maximum above its freezing point. At an oceanic average salinity of 35 grams per litre, seawater freezes at −1.8 °C, and the freezing temperature drops further with higher salinity and rises closer to 0 °C with lower salinity — which is why sea ice forms much more readily in low-salinity surface waters near river mouths or where summer melt has freshened the surface layer.

Critically, only the top layer of seawater needs to cool to its freezing point for ice to begin forming. This is the opposite of fresh water, where the whole column must approach 4 °C first, and it is the reason sea ice can form quickly across very large areas once conditions are right.

### New ice

The first stage in sea-ice formation is a population of needle-like ice crystals called frazil. Each crystal is three to four millimetres across. As they grow they expel salt back into the water around them — sea ice is, somewhat counterintuitively, made of nearly pure fresh water once mature — and the rejected brine sinks beneath the forming ice as cold, dense water. This brine rejection is one of the principal drivers of deep-water formation in the polar oceans.

Frazil crystals coalesce on the surface into a soupy layer called grease ice, which dampens small wave action and gives the sea a matt, oil-on-water appearance. In freshening conditions, grease ice can become slush — a viscous mass of partly frozen water and snow — or shuga, an accumulation of small white spongy lumps a few centimetres across. All of these are collectively termed new ice.

<figure class="lec-fig"><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><figcaption>New ice — frazil, grease, slush, and shuga. <span class="lec-fig__src">Morlighem et al. 2017.</span></figcaption></figure>

### Nilas and ice rind

Once new ice consolidates into a continuous sheet, it becomes nilas: a thin, elastic crust that bends visibly with the swell rather than breaking. Dark nilas is less than five centimetres thick and almost transparent against the dark water. Light nilas is between five and ten centimetres, lighter in appearance because it is opaque enough to scatter light. Ice rind is a slightly different form — a brittle, shiny crust up to five centimetres thick, formed in calm water with low salinity, which breaks rather than bends.

### Young ice

Young ice is the next developmental stage. It is divided into grey ice, ten to fifteen centimetres thick, and grey-white ice, fifteen to thirty centimetres thick. Both still have the elasticity of relatively young sea ice but no longer flex visibly with the swell.

### First-year ice

First-year ice is sea ice that has formed during a single winter and reached a thickness of between thirty centimetres and two metres. It is subdivided by thickness: thin first-year ice, sometimes called white ice, runs from thirty to seventy centimetres; medium first-year ice from seventy to one hundred and twenty centimetres; and thick first-year ice anything above that. Most of the Antarctic sea-ice cover is first-year ice, because almost all of it melts and reforms each year.

<figure class="lec-fig lec-fig--three"><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><figcaption>Thin, medium and thick first-year ice. <span class="lec-fig__src">Morlighem et al. 2017.</span></figcaption></figure>

### Old ice

Old ice is sea ice that has survived at least one summer melt season, and it is the substrate of the multi-year Arctic ice pack. Second-year ice has survived a single summer and may be 2.5 metres thick or more. Multi-year ice has survived at least two summers, runs to three metres or more in thickness, and — because successive summer melt has drained much of the original brine from its upper layers — looks distinctively bluer than younger ice. The decline of multi-year Arctic ice is one of the most consequential changes in the modern cryosphere; the central pack is now dominated by first-year ice in a way it was not a generation ago.

<figure class="lec-fig lec-fig--two"><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><figcaption>Old ice — second-year and multi-year. <span class="lec-fig__src">Morlighem et al. 2017.</span></figcaption></figure>

### Fast ice and pack ice

Sea ice is also classified by whether or not it can drift. Fast ice (or landfast ice) is sea ice that is anchored — attached to the shore, frozen to a glacier front, or grounded between shoals or stranded icebergs. It rises and falls with the tide but does not move horizontally. Fast ice may be just a few metres wide along a shoreline or extend several hundred kilometres seaward, as it does in parts of the Siberian and Canadian Arctic. The seaward boundary of the fast ice is the landfast ice edge; immediately beyond it, where fast ice meets drifting pack, is the flaw lead — a zone of recurring open water that is biologically critical and operationally important in equal measure.

Pack ice is everything else: drifting sea ice of any kind, in any concentration. It is what most polar mariners simply mean when they say “the ice.”

## Forms of floating ice

Within the pack itself, the WMO classification distinguishes a number of specific forms by size, shape and origin. These are not interchangeable terms, and learning to use them precisely is one of the practical things that separates a polar guide from a polar tourist.

### Pancake ice

Predominantly circular pieces of new ice, thirty centimetres to three metres across and up to ten centimetres thick, with characteristic raised rims caused by the pieces striking against each other on the swell. Pancakes form from grease ice, shuga or slush in water with active wave motion, and they can also form by the breaking-up of nilas, ice rind or even grey ice if the swell is strong enough. They occasionally form below the surface, at depth, where water masses of different physical properties meet, and float upward to consolidate. Pancake ice can cover very large areas of the Southern Ocean in autumn, and it is one of the most distinctive sights in early-season polar travel.

<figure class="lec-fig"><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><figcaption>Pancake ice — predominantly circular floes with raised rims. <span class="lec-fig__src">Morlighem et al. 2017.</span></figcaption></figure>

### Ice cake

A relatively flat piece of sea ice less than twenty metres across. A small ice cake is anything less than two metres across. Ice cakes are the smaller fragments left over from the breakup of larger structures or formed independently in calm conditions.

### Ice floe

Any relatively flat piece of sea ice twenty metres or more across. Floes are the principal building blocks of the pack and are subdivided by horizontal extent: small (twenty to one hundred metres across), medium (one hundred to five hundred metres), big (five hundred metres to two kilometres), vast (two to ten kilometres), and giant (more than ten kilometres). A giant floe in the central Arctic basin is, for practical purposes, a temporary island.

### Floeberg

A large piece of compounded sea ice — typically a hummock, or a group of hummocks frozen together — separated from the surrounding ice. A floeberg may protrude up to five metres above the sea surface and is composed entirely of sea ice, distinguishing it from a true iceberg, which is calved from glacier ice and originates on land.

<figure class="lec-fig lec-fig--three"><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><div class="lec-fig__img placeholder"></div><figcaption>Ice cake, ice floe, and floeberg. <span class="lec-fig__src">Morlighem et al. 2017.</span></figcaption></figure>

### Ice breccia

Pieces of sea ice from different stages of development, frozen together into a single composite mass. The name is borrowed from geology: a breccia, in rock, is a sedimentary mass of angular fragments cemented in a finer matrix. Ice breccia is the cryospheric equivalent — fragments of nilas, young ice, first-year ice and sometimes older ice, all welded into one body by a more recent freeze. It is among the more visually striking features of a winter pack, and it gives this lecture half of its title.

### Floebit

A small piece of compounded sea ice, usually not more than ten metres across, made up of one or more hummocks or part of a ridge frozen together and separated from any surrounding ice. Floebits typically protrude up to two metres above the surface and are essentially the small siblings of floebergs.

### Brash ice

An accumulation of fragments of floating ice, none more than two metres across. Brash is essentially the wreckage of other types of ice — the rubble produced when floes collide, when an iceberg disintegrates, or when a vessel breaks a path. A wake of brash, often persisting for hours behind a ship, is one of the few unambiguous signatures of human passage in a polar seascape.

## Sea ice as habitat

Sea ice is not simply a physical environment to be navigated. It is also a habitat, and one of the most productive on Earth in season.<span class="lec-cite">(6)</span>

The underside of multi-year sea ice supports communities of ice algae — specialised diatoms and other microalgae that grow within and beneath the ice itself, photosynthesising through the relatively translucent canopy above them. When the ice melts in spring, those algae are released into the water column along with a layer of fresh, nutrient-rich meltwater. The combination triggers blooms of phytoplankton on a scale that satellites can see from space.

The phytoplankton are the foundation of the entire polar marine food web. They feed copepods and krill; krill feed fish and the smaller cetaceans; the fish and krill in turn feed seals, seabirds, larger whales, and ultimately polar bears in the north and leopard seals and orca in the south. The annual rhythm of polar life — the timing of breeding, of migration, of pupping — is keyed to the rhythm of ice melt and the bloom that follows.
