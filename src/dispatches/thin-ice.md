## The ice has a vocabulary

Somewhere in the Arctic Ocean, on a night in late autumn, the sea stops looking like water.

The surface becomes dull. The wave crests lose their definition. The glinting, restless skin of the sea flattens into something thicker and slower, as though the ocean is considering becoming something else entirely. If you know what to look for, you can see it happen: the first crystals, tiny needles of pure water freezing out of the salt, rising to the surface and spreading into a thin, matte film that dampens the waves without stopping them. Grease ice, the guides call it. A beginning.

That beginning — molecule by molecule, crystal by crystal, until 14 or 15 million square kilometres of ocean are covered — is the Arctic sea ice maximum. It is the largest seasonal physical transformation on the surface of the Earth. It drives ocean circulation, structures whole food webs, defines the operational envelope for every ship that works in polar waters, and underwrites the cultural and subsistence lives of Inuit communities from Greenland to Alaska. It is not merely a number. But this year, the number tells a story.

On March 15, 2026, the Arctic sea ice maximum was recorded at 14.29 million square kilometres. The National Snow and Ice Data Center confirmed the figure: statistically tied with 2025 for the lowest maximum in the 48-year satellite record. The 2026 maximum fell 1.36 million square kilometres below the 1981–2010 average — an area, as the NSIDC put it, roughly equivalent to twice the size of Texas.

## How sea ice is born

Understanding what the 2026 maximum means requires understanding what sea ice actually is — not a frozen ocean, not a simple extension of winter weather, but a structured, living material that forms through a precise sequence of physical transformations, each with its own properties, its own visual signature, and its own consequences for the world around it.

Fresh water freezes at 0°C. Seawater, loaded with dissolved salts at roughly 35 parts per thousand, does not freeze until approximately −1.8°C. Before it can reach even that threshold, the ocean must shed the heat stored throughout its surface layer — a slow, convective process that can take weeks of sustained autumn cooling.

> See how the ocean surface looks greasy and dull, as though someone poured oil on it? Those are the first ice crystals forming, tiny needles of pure water freezing out of the saltwater. The ocean is at minus 1.8 degrees right now. By tomorrow morning, this could be a solid sheet.
>
> — Ice in Practice, Chapter 6 (Henry Páll Wulff, manuscript in preparation)

As freezing continues, the grease consolidates into nilas — a thin, elastic sheet, almost black when less than five centimetres thick because the dark water beneath shows through. In areas of wave action, which is the norm in the open Southern Ocean and common at the margins of the Arctic, the story takes a different path: the frazil aggregates not into a continuous sheet but into circular discs, typically 30 centimetres to three metres across, with raised rims from repeated collisions. Pancake ice. One of the most visually extraordinary features of the polar ocean, and one of the most reliable indicators that the marginal ice zone is in active formation.

Eventually, as the ice thickens beyond 30 centimetres, it becomes first-year ice: the white, snow-covered sheet that constitutes the bulk of both polar ice packs every winter. If it survives a full summer melt season, it becomes something else entirely — multi-year ice, denser and harder than the year before, with air bubbles and brine channels slowly draining from its lattice over years of freeze and melt, until the ice is purer, denser, stronger. And blue. Blue where first-year ice is white, because the loss of those internal scatterers allows the ice to absorb red wavelengths and transmit blue — the same optical process that makes deep glacier ice appear to glow from within.

> Notice the colour difference between those two floes. The white one formed this winter. The blue one has survived at least one summer melt. Over time, the salt drains out, the bubbles compress, and the ice becomes denser, harder, and bluer. That blue floe might be five or ten years old.
>
> — Ice in Practice, Chapter 6 (Henry Páll Wulff, manuscript in preparation)

Blue ice is old ice. Old ice is strong ice. And in the Arctic today, old ice is vanishing.

## The numbers of March 15, 2026

February 2026 offered little comfort to anyone watching the numbers. Rick Thoman's Alaska and Arctic Climate Newsletter, among the most authoritative running commentaries on conditions in the high north, tracked the month's progression: sea ice extent running near the bottom of the historical range throughout February, with the Bering Sea persistently and anomalously bare of ice. The Copernicus Climate Change Service confirmed the picture at the pan-Arctic scale: extent among the lowest values in the satellite record, consistent with an ocean that had retained too much summer heat, frozen too late, and formed ice too thin to survive the season ahead.

The maximum arrived on March 15 and was not approached again. At 14.29 million square kilometres, it matched — within the margin of measurement — the 2025 record. The two values are, in the NSIDC's own assessment, a statistical tie: the lowest maximum in the 48-year record, recorded in back-to-back years.

> This record low maximum gives a head start to the spring and summer melt season. One or two record low years don't necessarily mean much by themselves, but in the context of the significant downward trend that we've observed since 1979, it reinforces the dramatic change to Arctic sea ice throughout all seasons.
>
> — Walt Meier, senior research scientist, NSIDC

The caveat is important and Meier is right to insist on it. A single year's maximum extent does not, by itself, constitute a trend. What makes the 2026 figure significant is not its isolation but its position: at the bottom of a 48-year distribution that has been moving steadily downward since the late 1970s. The September minimum extent — the Arctic's summer floor — has been declining at roughly 13 per cent per decade. The maximum has been declining too, more slowly but unmistakably.

## Pikialasorsuaq: a world held open by a bridge

Between the northwest coast of Greenland and the eastern shore of Ellesmere Island, at 76 to 79 degrees north latitude, lies a body of water that should not exist. By meteorological logic, it should be frozen solid throughout the winter. It sits deep in the High Arctic, surrounded by some of the thickest sea ice on the planet, subjected to temperatures that routinely fall below −30°C for months at a time. And yet it remains open — or thinly iced — throughout the cold season, covering up to 80,000 square kilometres of some of the most productive marine water in the Arctic.

Its Inuktitut name is Pikialasorsuaq: the great upwelling. The name is not a poetic flourish. It is a piece of knowledge. Warm water rising from depth, combined with an ice bridge that forms across Nares Strait each winter and blocks the southward drift of pack ice, holds the polynya open — and with it, the entire ecosystem that depends on winter access to the sea.

## What the ice tells us

The sea ice record is not an academic exercise. It informs shipping routes and maritime safety. It underpins the climate models used by every government that is planning for sea level rise, extreme weather, and agricultural disruption. It is the foundational evidence for international climate negotiations. And it belongs, in the deepest sense, to the communities that have lived on and around Arctic sea ice for thousands of years and who have the most direct and immediate stake in understanding its future.

There is a place in the High Arctic, north of Ellesmere Island and along the northern coast of Greenland, that researchers have designated the Last Ice Area. The name is not a hopeful one. It is a projection — a best estimate, based on ocean geometry, ice dynamics, and climate modelling, of where the Arctic's summer sea ice will make its last stand as temperatures continue to rise.

Reading that story requires knowing the difference between a white floe and a blue one. It requires listening for the groan of compression, reading the colour of open water against cloud base, understanding what the raised rim of a pressure ridge says about the forces that built it, and what the name Pikialasorsuaq says about the knowledge of the people who gave it. It requires, in the fullest sense, translation.

The ice is always telling its story. The guide's role is translation.
