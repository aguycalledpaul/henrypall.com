## The ice has a vocabulary

Somewhere in the Arctic Ocean, on a night in late autumn, the sea stops looking like water.

The surface becomes dull. The wave crests lose their definition. The glinting, restless skin of the sea flattens into something thicker and slower, as though the ocean is considering becoming something else entirely. If you know what to look for, you can see it happen: the first crystals, tiny needles of pure water freezing out of the salt, rising to the surface and spreading into a thin, matte film that dampens the waves without stopping them. Grease ice, the guides call it. A beginning.

That beginning — molecule by molecule, crystal by crystal, until 14 or 15 million square kilometres of ocean are covered — is the Arctic sea ice maximum. It is the largest seasonal physical transformation on the surface of the Earth. It drives ocean circulation, structures whole food webs, defines the operational envelope for every ship that works in polar waters, and underwrites the cultural and subsistence lives of Inuit communities from Greenland to Alaska. It is not merely a number. But this year, the number tells a story.

On March 15, 2026, the Arctic sea ice maximum was recorded at 14.29 million square kilometres. The National Snow and Ice Data Center confirmed the figure: statistically tied with 2025 for the lowest maximum in the 48-year satellite record.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="1" href="#ref-1">(1)</a> The previous record before that had stood since March 7, 2017, when the ice reached only 14.42 million square kilometres and shocked climate scientists at the time. Within a decade, that shock had become a floor. The 2026 maximum fell 1.36 million square kilometres below the 1981–2010 average — an area, as the NSIDC put it, roughly equivalent to twice the size of Texas.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="2" href="#ref-2">(2)</a>

This is what that area looked like: ocean surface, exposed to the Arctic winter, absorbing heat, exchanging gases with the atmosphere, responding to storms — where, in a historical average year, it would have been sealed under ice.

## How sea ice is born

Understanding what the 2026 maximum means requires understanding what sea ice actually is — not a frozen ocean, not a simple extension of winter weather, but a structured, living material that forms through a precise sequence of physical transformations, each with its own properties, its own visual signature, and its own consequences for the world around it.

Fresh water freezes at 0°C. Seawater, loaded with dissolved salts at roughly 35 parts per thousand, does not freeze until approximately −1.8°C. Before it can reach even that threshold, the ocean must shed the heat stored throughout its surface layer — a slow, convective process that can take weeks of sustained autumn cooling. The first ice crystals — frazil — are tiny, needle-shaped, no more than a few millimetres across. They rise to the surface and aggregate into a thin, soupy layer that gives the ocean a distinctly dull, viscous appearance, dampening small waves without stopping them. This is grease ice, and it is unmistakable to anyone who knows what to look for.

> See how the ocean surface looks greasy and dull, as though someone poured oil on it? Those are the first ice crystals forming, tiny needles of pure water freezing out of the saltwater. The ocean is at minus 1.8 degrees right now. By tomorrow morning, this could be a solid sheet.
>
> — Ice in Practice, Chapter 6 (Henry Páll Wulff, manuscript in preparation)<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="3" href="#ref-3">(3)</a>

The dull, matte surface is a thin suspension of frazil crystals, tens of thousands of them per litre. <span class="ds-fig__src">Henry Páll Wulff, Pia Harboure</span></figcaption></figure>

As freezing continues, the grease consolidates into nilas — a thin, elastic sheet, almost black when less than five centimetres thick because the dark water beneath shows through. In areas of wave action, which is the norm in the open Southern Ocean and common at the margins of the Arctic, the story takes a different path: the frazil aggregates not into a continuous sheet but into circular discs, typically 30 centimetres to three metres across, with raised rims from repeated collisions. Pancake ice. One of the most visually extraordinary features of the polar ocean, and one of the most reliable indicators that the marginal ice zone is in active formation.

Eventually, as the ice thickens beyond 30 centimetres, it becomes first-year ice: the white, snow-covered sheet that constitutes the bulk of both polar ice packs every winter. If it survives a full summer melt season, it becomes something else entirely — multi-year ice, denser and harder than the year before, with air bubbles and brine channels slowly draining from its lattice over years of freeze and melt, until the ice is purer, denser, stronger. And blue. Blue where first-year ice is white, because the loss of those internal scatterers allows the ice to absorb red wavelengths and transmit blue — the same optical process that makes deep glacier ice appear to glow from within.

> Notice the colour difference between those two floes. The white one formed this winter. The blue one has survived at least one summer melt. Over time, the salt drains out, the bubbles compress, and the ice becomes denser, harder, and bluer. That blue floe might be five or ten years old.
>
> — Ice in Practice, Chapter 6 (Henry Páll Wulff, manuscript in preparation)<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="4" href="#ref-4">(4)</a>

Blue ice is old ice. Old ice is strong ice. And in the Arctic today, old ice is vanishing.

## The numbers of March 15, 2026

February 2026 offered little comfort to anyone watching the numbers. Rick Thoman's Alaska and Arctic Climate Newsletter, among the most authoritative running commentaries on conditions in the high north, tracked the month's progression: sea ice extent running near the bottom of the historical range throughout February, with the Bering Sea persistently and anomalously bare of ice.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="5" href="#ref-5">(5)</a> The Copernicus Climate Change Service confirmed the picture at the pan-Arctic scale: extent among the lowest values in the satellite record, consistent with an ocean that had retained too much summer heat, frozen too late, and formed ice too thin to survive the season ahead.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="6" href="#ref-6">(6)</a>

The maximum arrived on March 15 and was not approached again.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="7" href="#ref-7">(7)</a> At 14.29 million square kilometres, it matched — within the margin of measurement — the 2025 record. The two values are, in the NSIDC's own assessment, a statistical tie: the lowest maximum in the 48-year record, recorded in back-to-back years.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="8" href="#ref-8">(8)</a>

> This record low maximum gives a head start to the spring and summer melt season. One or two record low years don't necessarily mean much by themselves, but in the context of the significant downward trend that we've observed since 1979, it reinforces the dramatic change to Arctic sea ice throughout all seasons.
>
> — Walt Meier, senior research scientist, NSIDC<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="9" href="#ref-9">(9)</a>

The caveat is important and Meier is right to insist on it. A single year's maximum extent does not, by itself, constitute a trend. What makes the 2026 figure significant is not its isolation but its position: at the bottom of a 48-year distribution that has been moving steadily downward since the late 1970s. The September minimum extent — the Arctic's summer floor — has been declining at roughly 13 per cent per decade.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="10" href="#ref-10">(10)</a> The maximum has been declining too, more slowly but unmistakably.

## Pikialasorsuaq: a world held open by a bridge

Between the northwest coast of Greenland and the eastern shore of Ellesmere Island, at 76 to 79 degrees north latitude, lies a body of water that should not exist. By meteorological logic, it should be frozen solid throughout the winter. It sits deep in the High Arctic, surrounded by some of the thickest sea ice on the planet, subjected to temperatures that routinely fall below −30°C for months at a time. And yet it remains open — or thinly iced — throughout the cold season, covering up to 80,000 square kilometres of some of the most productive marine water in the Arctic.

Its Inuktitut name is Pikialasorsuaq: the great upwelling. The name is not a poetic flourish. It is a piece of knowledge. It reflects an understanding — accumulated over millennia of observation and travel across the ice — of the ocean processes that maintain this feature. Western science gave it the descriptor "polynya" only in the mid-twentieth century. The Inuit knew it, named it, and built their lives around it far longer than that.

The mechanism that maintains the polynya is elegant. Each winter, an ice bridge forms across the northern end of Nares Strait, the narrow channel connecting the Lincoln Sea to Baffin Bay. This bridge — composed of thick multi-year ice that locks together across the 35 kilometre gap between Greenland and Ellesmere Island — blocks the southward flow of Arctic Ocean ice into the polynya region. South of the bridge, strong northerly winds push the locally formed ice away from the northern shores, continuously exposing new ocean surface to the frigid air. The water freezes, the new ice is pushed south by the wind, and the cycle repeats throughout the winter. The polynya is maintained not by any source of warmth but by the perpetual removal of ice — a factory of open water running through the coldest months of the year.

> That pile of broken ice blocks, the ridge running across the floe? Two enormous pieces of ice were pushed together by the wind, and neither would give way, so the ice buckled and shattered upward. What you see above the surface is only a quarter of it. Below the waterline, it extends three to four times deeper.
>
> — Ice in Practice, Chapter 6 (Henry Páll Wulff, manuscript in preparation)<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="11" href="#ref-11">(11)</a>

The pressure ridges that gather at the margins of Pikialasorsuaq are not incidental scenery. They are visible evidence of the forces that maintain the feature — the compression of ice against the bridge to the north, the convergence of drifting floes at the polynya's edges. Every ridge records a collision. Every keel plunging four metres or more below the waterline is a monument to the energy of moving ice.

The ecological consequences of this open-water factory are extraordinary. With the surrounding pack ice locked under darkness and cold, the polynya allows sunlight to penetrate months before it can reach the ocean beneath the surrounding pack, fuelling phytoplankton blooms that sustain one of the richest marine food webs anywhere in the polar north. Copepods and Arctic cod feed on the plankton; ringed seals, Arctic char, and narwhal feed on the cod. Beluga whales, walrus, and bowhead whales congregate here in numbers found in few other Arctic locations. The North Water supports an estimated 80 per cent of all dovekies in the North Atlantic.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="12" href="#ref-12">(12)</a> Polar bears work the ice edge. Pikialasorsuaq is, in the precise ecological sense, an oasis — and the world's most productive one at this latitude.

For the Inughuit of northwest Greenland and the Inuit of the Qikiqtaaluk region on the Canadian side, the polynya has been a source of food, livelihood, and cultural identity for thousands of years. The seasonal rhythms of the polynya — when it opens, when the ice bridge holds, when conditions are safe for travel and for crossing — constitute a body of knowledge as sophisticated as anything that modern remote sensing can produce. The Inuit Circumpolar Council's Pikialasorsuaq Commission, established in 2016, was an explicit assertion of this: Indigenous governance of the polynya is not simply a cultural aspiration but a knowledge claim grounded in thousands of years of authoritative observation.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="13" href="#ref-13">(13)</a>

## A living platform

For most of human history, sea ice was imagined as inert — a frozen desert, hostile and featureless, something to be endured or avoided. The science of the past few decades has replaced that image with something far more intricate.

A 2023 study published in Nature Communications — one of the most comprehensive syntheses of Arctic ecosystem dependency ever attempted — examined the food-web relationships of 155 Arctic species, from microscopic algae to polar bears, to sea ice-associated carbon: the organic matter produced by ice-associated phytoplankton and algae and distributed through the food web as ice forms, melts, and degrades each season.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="14" href="#ref-14">(14)</a> The results were stark. Ice algal carbon signatures were present in 96 per cent of the more than 2,300 samples collected, across all 155 species investigated, across all months of the year — from January to December.

Sea ice, in other words, is not a seasonal platform that the Arctic ecosystem uses in winter and abandons in summer. It is the primary carbon source that structures the entire food web, its productive pulse propagating through copepods, fish, seals, and whales across months and seasons, long after the ice itself has retreated. The system depends on the ice forming, and forming on time, and forming in sufficient quantity to generate the carbon pulse that everything downstream relies upon.

The productive pulse initiated by ice algae in spring cascades through the food web for months. <span class="ds-fig__src">After Koch et al. (2023)</span></figcaption></figure>

This is the biological reality that the raw number — 14.29 million square kilometres — does not convey. The sea ice that is absent is not empty space. It is, or was, a platform for life: for algae that colonise the underside of the ice in spring, for the copepods that graze on those algae, for the fish that feed on the copepods, for the seals and whales and bears and birds that have organised their entire existence around the presence of that frozen surface. As the ice retreats earlier in spring and forms later in autumn, the timing of the ice-associated productivity pulse shifts, potentially decoupling it from the life cycles of the animals that depend on it.

## Pikialasorsuaq under pressure

The ice bridge across Nares Strait — the mechanism on which the entire polynya depends — is under threat.

As Arctic temperatures rise at approximately four times the global average rate,<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="15" href="#ref-15">(15)</a> the multi-year ice that has historically locked together across the strait is thinning. The bridge has been forming later in autumn and breaking up earlier in spring. In some recent years, it has failed to form at all, allowing Arctic ice to flow freely south through Nares Strait and fundamentally altering the character and persistence of the polynya. The consequences for the ecosystem — for the narwhal, the walrus, the dovekies, the phytoplankton, the entire productive cascade — are not yet fully quantified, but the direction is unambiguous.

For the Inuit communities of the region, this is not a projection or a model output. It is observed, lived, and transmitted in real time through the same networks of knowledge that have tracked Pikialasorsuaq's rhythms for thousands of years. The SIKU platform — a digital system developed by the Arctic Eider Society to collect and share ice observations from Inuit hunters and travellers across the Arctic — documents these changes in the language and categories of Inuit knowledge, carrying the semantic content of terms like sikuliaq (young ice forming, a temporal warning as much as a physical description), qinu (slushy, waterlogged ice, dangerously weak), and ivuniq (a pressure ridge, encoded not as a geographic feature but as a practical decision: can this be crossed?).<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="16" href="#ref-16">(16)</a> The SmartICE programme, developed in partnership with Inuit communities in Nunatsiavut and expanded across the Canadian Arctic, goes further still: combining traditional ice knowledge with modern sensor technology to produce real-time ice safety data that neither approach could generate alone.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="17" href="#ref-17">(17)</a>

> Listen to the ice. That groaning sound? The pack is being compressed by the wind. Millions of tonnes of ice adjusting, grinding, building pressure. When you hear that sound, the ice is doing work.
>
> — Ice in Practice, Chapter 6 (Henry Páll Wulff, manuscript in preparation)<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="18" href="#ref-18">(18)</a>

The Pikialasorsuaq Commission's 2017 report, People of the Ice Bridge: The Future of the Pikialasorsuaq, called explicitly for Indigenous-led governance of the polynya region and warned of mounting pressure from climate change, shipping, and resource extraction.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="19" href="#ref-19">(19)</a> Nine years on, the ice bridge is less reliable, the ice that forms it is younger and thinner, and the 2026 record maximum adds one more data point to the trajectory the Commission documented.

## The ice that remembers

There is another story embedded in the 2026 maximum that the headline figure alone does not tell. In the early 1980s, ice older than four years covered roughly 30 per cent of the Arctic Ocean. Today, such ice has all but disappeared. Multi-year ice as a whole — once comprising half or more of the Arctic ice pack — now constitutes less than 20 per cent, and its decline has accelerated in recent years.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="20" href="#ref-20">(20)</a> The record-low maximum of March 2026 is, in a very precise sense, a record-low maximum of thin, young, first-year ice.

This distinction matters for reasons that go far beyond symbolism. Multi-year ice is the ice that remembers. Over years of freeze and melt cycles, brine drains from its internal lattice, leaving behind a denser, harder, bluer material that resists wind forcing, insulates the ocean below, and moves through the Arctic like a slow, massive flywheel — dampening the variability of the entire system. First-year ice is its structural opposite: thin, porous, saline, and exquisitely responsive to every weather event. An Arctic dominated by first-year ice is an Arctic whose ice cover can open and close, compress and disperse, advance and retreat on timescales of days rather than weeks or months.

For the ecosystems that co-evolved with multi-year ice, the shift is equally disruptive. Ringed seals use stable multi-year ice as a pupping platform. Polar bears depend on sea ice to access their primary prey, and the decline of multi-year ice in the central Arctic has progressively altered their seasonal movements and energy balance.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="21" href="#ref-21">(21)</a> An Arctic that runs on first-year ice is not a colder version of an Arctic without ice. It is a different ecosystem.

## The instruments we are switching off

In a development that many in the polar science community regard as alarming in its own right — separate from but compounding the data it was designed to collect — the monitoring infrastructure that makes the sea ice record possible has faced significant financial and institutional pressure in recent years.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="22" href="#ref-22">(22)</a> The NSIDC, based at the University of Colorado Boulder and responsible for maintaining the sea ice index that produced the 2026 record, has operated under budget constraints that have threatened the continuity of analysis capacity. The satellite programme that generates the underlying data requires continuous investment in both hardware and the scientific expertise to interpret it.

There is a particular cruelty to this coincidence. At the moment when the Arctic is changing faster than at any point in the observational record — when the gap between the historical average and current reality is widest, when the data is most urgently needed by scientists, planners, coastal communities, and governments trying to understand what is happening to their world — this is precisely the moment at which the infrastructure for generating that data is under strain.

The sea ice record is not an academic exercise. It informs shipping routes and maritime safety. It underpins the climate models used by every government that is planning for sea level rise, extreme weather, and agricultural disruption. It is the foundational evidence for international climate negotiations. Switching off the instruments while the experiment is running is not a budget decision. It is a choice about what kind of knowledge the world will have access to in the decades ahead.

## What the ice tells us

There is a place in the High Arctic, north of Ellesmere Island and along the northern coast of Greenland, that researchers have designated the Last Ice Area.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="23" href="#ref-23">(23)</a> The name is not a hopeful one. It is a projection — a best estimate, based on ocean geometry, ice dynamics, and climate modelling, of where the Arctic's summer sea ice will make its last stand as temperatures continue to rise. Thick multi-year ice, pushed against the northern coasts of the archipelago and Greenland by the Transpolar Drift, accumulates here in concentrations that may persist into summer even as the rest of the Arctic Basin becomes seasonally ice-free.

For the species that depend on summer sea ice — polar bears, walrus, ice-associated seals — the Last Ice Area may become the final refuge. For Inuit communities, it represents both a place of hope and a place of dread: hope, because the ecosystem may endure there when it fails elsewhere; dread, because what it means for the ice to have a last area is that everywhere else, it will have gone.

The 2026 maximum sits within a trajectory that does not point upward. The September minimum extent has been declining at roughly 13 per cent per decade since the satellite record began.<a class="ds-cite" data-ref="24" href="#ref-24">(24)</a> The warming of the Arctic Ocean — which retains more summer heat in open water and releases it back to the atmosphere in autumn, delaying freeze-up — is a feedback that accelerates its own cause. A warmer Arctic is harder to freeze, and an Arctic that is harder to freeze stays warmer. The 2026 record is not a dramatic outlier from an otherwise stable baseline. It is a milestone on the most consequential physical trajectory currently unfolding on the surface of the Earth.

What the record also makes visible is the inadequacy of extent as a metric for what has been lost. The ice that is absent above the median line is not equivalent to the ice that remains below it. The ice that remains is younger, thinner, more volatile, and less capable of sustaining the biological and physical functions that polar sea ice has performed for millions of years. The story of Arctic sea ice is not only a story of shrinking area. It is a story of the replacement of one kind of ice — old, dense, blue, remembered — with another: young, thin, white, and provisional.

Reading that story requires knowing the difference between a white floe and a blue one. It requires listening for the groan of compression, reading the colour of open water against cloud base, understanding what the raised rim of a pressure ridge says about the forces that built it, and what the name Pikialasorsuaq says about the knowledge of the people who gave it. It requires, in the fullest sense, translation.

The ice is always telling its story. The guide's role is translation.
